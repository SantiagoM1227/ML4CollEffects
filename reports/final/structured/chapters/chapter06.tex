
\section{Discussion and perspectives}
\label{sec:discussion}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Exploratory research and inductive-bias discovery}
This work was exploratory in the sense that the final architecture was not fixed at the beginning. Several models were investigated to understand which representations and inductive biases are appropriate for high-dimensional beam-distribution evolution. This should be interpreted as a strength of the project: the comparison between FNO, Transformer, Window Transformer, and GNO revealed a coherent pattern. Models that respect the operator and locality structure of beam dynamics produce more stable and physically consistent predictions than unconstrained global sequence models.

The thesis should therefore not be read as a catalogue of architectures. It should be read as an inductive-bias study: the same physical problem is approached with spectral operators, latent sequence models, local attention, and graph kernels, and the results identify locality as the main structural requirement.

\subsection{Why locality matters in beam dynamics}
Beam evolution in an accelerator is a composition of local lattice transformations and collective interactions with a causal structure along the bunch and along the lattice. Even when wakefields introduce nonlocality, that nonlocality is structured: it is not arbitrary all-to-all mixing. In particular, head--tail interactions are directed because trailing particles respond to fields generated by leading particles. Architectures that ignore this directionality can fit training data but may accumulate biased errors during rollout.

The windowed Transformer and the GNO both reduce this problem. The windowed Transformer constrains the support of the attention kernel. The GNO uses an explicit neighborhood graph and learned kernel. The similarity of their results is therefore physically meaningful: both approximate a localized transport kernel, one implicitly through attention masking and the other explicitly through message passing.

\subsection{Uncertainty quantification with conditional latent models}
The CVAE formulation provides a natural opening toward uncertainty quantification. For fixed conditioning variables $c$, one may sample several latent variables
\begin{equation}
    z^{(r)}\sim q_{\phi}(z\mid X,c),\qquad r=1,\ldots,R,
\end{equation}
and propagate them through the learned dynamics. This produces an ensemble of predictions
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{X}_{N}^{(r)}=\mathcal{D}_{\psi}\big(\mathcal{T}_{\theta}^{N}(z^{(r)},\mu),c\big),
\end{equation}
from which predictive means, variances, and confidence intervals can be estimated. Such uncertainty estimates would be useful for identifying regions of parameter space where the surrogate is unreliable, where the training data are sparse, or where the beam response is intrinsically sensitive.

\subsection{Toward accelerator digital twins and optimization}
Fast surrogate models are useful not only for reproducing simulation outputs but also for enabling downstream tasks. Once trained, a differentiable surrogate can be used for sensitivity analysis, Bayesian optimization, uncertainty-aware parameter scans, and eventually online model calibration. In this sense, the present work fits into the broader development of accelerator digital twins: fast, approximate, continuously updated models that complement high-fidelity simulations and measurements.

A natural future direction is to combine the learned surrogate with Bayesian optimization. The surrogate could provide fast evaluations of beam quality metrics, while a higher-level optimizer explores machine settings under constraints. This is especially promising for expensive simulation studies and for online tuning scenarios where direct exploration is limited by machine time or operational safety \cite{roussel_bayesian_2024}.

\subsection{Limitations}
Several limitations should be stated clearly. First, the surrogate is only as reliable as the training distribution. Generalization outside the sampled parameter range cannot be assumed. Second, the 15-projection representation is informative but not equivalent to the full six-dimensional density; distinct distributions may share the same pairwise marginals. Third, the CVAE introduces stochasticity and compression, which may smooth fine filamentary structures. Fourth, the current models do not exactly enforce symplecticity, charge conservation, or known wakefield causality constraints. Finally, the results must be validated against increasingly realistic collective-effects simulations before operational use.

These limitations do not invalidate the approach. Rather, they define the next research steps: richer data, physics-constrained kernels, uncertainty-aware validation, and comparisons against high-fidelity simulations over broader parameter domains.
